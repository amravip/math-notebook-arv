2Learn

A transformation moves or resizes a shape: translation (slide), reflection (flip), rotation (turn), enlargement (scale). Coordinates change predictably.

A transformation moves or resizes a shape. The starting shape is the object, the shape you end up with is the image, and matching corners are labelled A and A′ ("A dash").

The four transformations

TransformationWhat it doesYou must stateSize
Translationslides every point the same distance in the same directionthe vector — how far right/left, how far up/downunchanged
Reflectionflips the shape over a mirror line; each point lands the same distance the other sidethe mirror line (e.g. the y-axis, or y = x)unchanged
Rotationturns the shape about a fixed pointthe centre, the angle, and the directionunchanged
Enlargementstretches or shrinks the shape away from a centrethe centre and the scale factor k× k
  • Translation, reflection and rotation are rigid motions: every length and every angle survives, so the image is congruent (identical, just moved). Only its position or facing changes.
  • An enlargement keeps all the angles but multiplies every length, so the image is similar — the same shape, a different size. For example, a triangle enlarged by scale factor 2 keeps its angles but every side is twice as long.
  • If a question does not say which way to turn, mathematicians rotate anticlockwise by convention. So "rotate 90°" with no direction given means 90° anticlockwise.

Lengths scale by k — but area scales by k²

  • An enlargement multiplies every length by the scale factor k. For example, scale factor 3 turns a 2 cm side into 6 cm.
  • Area does not scale by k — it scales by k², because a shape is stretched in two directions at once. For example, scale factor 3 turns an area of 5 cm² into 5 × 3² = 45 cm², not 15.
  • So doubling every side (k = 2) makes a shape hold 4 times as much, not twice. For example, a 3 × 3 square (area 9) enlarges to 6 × 6, area 36 — four times as much.
  • The same logic carries on: volume scales by k³. One, two, three dimensions — one, two, three powers of k.

Transforming a shape, point by point

  1. Read what is fixed: a translation needs its vector, a reflection its mirror line, a rotation its centre and direction, an enlargement its centre and scale factor. A rotation without a centre is not a defined move.
  2. Take the vertices one at a time and apply the rule to each, writing the image coordinates down as you go. Never try to move the whole shape by eye.
  3. Join the image points in the same order as the original, and label them A′, B′, C′.
  4. Check the result is the right kind of shape: after a slide, flip or turn it must be congruent — same size, same angles. Only an enlargement may change the size.
  5. Sanity-check one length against the original. If a "rotation" has changed a side length, a point was plotted wrongly.

🎮 Video game characters

A game character flips to face left instead of right — a reflection. It slides across the screen — a translation. Its size never changes in either move, only its position or facing direction.

🖨️ Enlarging on a photocopier

A photocopier enlarges a photo by 150% (scale factor 1.5). Every length grows by 1.5×, but the paper's area grows by 1.5² = 2.25×, not 1.5×.

🧩 Wallpaper patterns

A repeating wallpaper pattern is built entirely from translations — sliding the same motif sideways and down, no resizing or flipping needed, to tile the whole wall.

Why area behaves differently: scaling lengths by k scales area by k². Double every side and the shape holds four times the space, not two.
⚠ Watch outEnlarging by scale factor 2 makes the area 4× bigger (2²), not 2×. Lengths and areas scale differently.
