Pythagoras' theorem
a² + b² = c²

In a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides: a² + b² = c².

In a right-angled triangle, the side opposite the right angle is always the longest, and has its own name: the hypotenuse. Find the right angle first, look straight across from it, and that is the hypotenuse — whichever way the triangle happens to be turned.

Why the rule talks about squares

Pythagoras' theorem: a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse and a and b are the two shorter sides. It works for every right-angled triangle and for no other kind, so the first thing to check is that the right-angle mark is really there.

Two jobs — and the difference is add or subtract

  • Missing hypotenuse? Add. c = √(a² + b²). For example, legs 6 and 8: c = √(36 + 64) = √100 = 10.
  • Missing shorter side? Subtract. a = √(c² − b²), always taking the hypotenuse's square first. For example, hypotenuse 13 and one leg 5: a = √(169 − 25) = √144 = 12.
  • Self-check: the hypotenuse must be the longest side. If a shorter side comes out bigger than the hypotenuse, you added when you should have subtracted.

Working through a question

  1. Mark the right angle and label the side opposite it c.
  2. Decide the job: is c the missing one (add the squares) or a known one (subtract)?
  3. Square, combine, then square-root.
  4. Round only at the end, and write the units. Keep the full decimal through the middle steps — rounding early shifts the final answer.
  • Some side lengths come out whole. Sets like 3-4-5, 5-12-13 and 8-15-17 fit exactly and are worth recognising instantly. Multiplying a set through still works: double 3-4-5 for 6-8-10, triple it for 9-12-15.
  • The converse works too: if a² + b² = c² is true for a triangle's three sides, then that triangle must have a right angle. The theorem can test a triangle, not just measure one. For example, 8-15-17: 64 + 225 = 289 = 17², so the corner is exactly square.
  • When the answer is not whole, leave it as a rounded decimal with units. For example, √20 ≈ 4.472 — carry those decimals, and write 4.5 cm only on the final line.

🪜 Setting up a ladder

A 5 m ladder leans against a wall with its base 3 m out. The ladder is the hypotenuse, so this is a subtract job: it reaches √(5² − 3²) = 4 m up the wall — a 3-4-5 triangle.

📺 A TV's screen size

A TV advertised as "55 inches" means the diagonal of the screen (the hypotenuse) is 55 inches — not its width or height, which form the two shorter sides.

📐 Checking a corner is square

Builders mark 3 m along one wall and 4 m along the other from a corner. If the diagonal between those marks measures exactly 5 m, the converse guarantees the corner is a true right angle.

Why it matters: it links all three sides, so knowing two always finds the third — used in construction, navigation and screen sizes.
⚠ Watch outThe theorem works only for right-angled triangles, and c must be the hypotenuse — the longest side, opposite the right angle.
